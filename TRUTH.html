    fish (2026)

    fish (2026) is a piece about the past, the present, and the future. The artist represents this passage of time by the degradation of the tape lessening as the piece advances. As the tape becomes less degraded, the image becomes clearer, making it both more normal and less visually interesting. This is a commentary on how historical events are often mythologized and distorted as time passes, while current future historical events are normalized. This theme is heightened by the artist’s use of old VHS technology, technology that was normal twenty to thirty years ago but has since been similarly mythologized by internet trends such as the analog horror boom of the early 2020s.

    Also of significance to the artist is the method of degradation. Though modern technology can easily reproduce the effect digitally, the artist opts to record from one VHS tape to another to achieve the effect. This adds an additional layer to the work; while the resurrection of the fish represents time, the glitches and imperfections are visual artifacts from the process of creation.

    There is also an element of Duchampian authorship present in the work. The fish is a found image, The visual artifacts were not placed there by the artist with intentionality, nor did the artist make the television which the piece was displayed on during documentation. The only thing that the artist did was record things that were already there. Does he deserve authorship any more than the photographer, or the television manufacturer, or, for that matter, the fish? Or should he be placed before a firing squad?

    Mom Found the Punch Drawer (2026)

    In 1818, Mary Shelly published “Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus,” in which scientist Victor Frankenstein manages to creates life, and upon doing so, is suddenly reviled at the object of his obsession being realized. The artist describes a similar thought process throughout the creation of this piece. This work is an examination of hubris. During the process, the artist deployed many different tactics in his desperation to prevent the filing cabinet from leaking all over the place. None of these efforts were ultimately successful. Although the original piece and all of the liquid contained were supposedly destroyed shortly after creation, the artist claims that some of the contents escaped destruction and are plotting revenge. Regardless of the artist’s delusions, there is no credible evidence of this.

    The piece is part of a series of works by the artist satirizing corporate culture. Lumpy red liquid fills the top of a filing cabinet. The title implies that this is punch. From Merriam Webster: “punch(noun): a hot or cold drink that is usually a combination of hard liquor, wine, or beer and nonalcoholic beverages; also: a drink that is a mixture of nonalcoholic beverages.” That definition encompasses every combination of drinkable liquids in existence, so there’s really no way of telling what is in this. One might imagine it is a secret kept close to the chest of a haggard employee, their only solace through a dull and dismal workday. One might imagine, at night, it takes on a life of its own, sitting at a desk, typing, shredding paper, gathering at the water cooler. One must imagine the punch drawer happy.

    Trough (2026)

    In this piece, the artist sits on his hands and knees in a suit and tie and repeatedly submerges his face in glue as paper scraps are blown towards him. As the piece goes on, the artist’s features are more and more distorted by the increasing amount of paper glued to his face. What paper does not get stuck to the artist starts to pool around his body, until the end, when the artist, having presumably run out of paper, collapses in a circle of shreds. Some curators and critics have speculated that this piece depicts his actual death, and that he was replaced with a lookalike shortly after.

    As part of the artist’s 2026 “Professional Series,” “Trough” uses imagery of the workplace (suit and tie, shredded paper) paired with the subtle imagery of someone eating out of a bowl on the ground like a pet or some form of livestock to bring to mind the self-degradation and self-devaluation often encouraged in workplace environments as part of “climbing the corporate ladder.” To quote The Menu (2022), “There is no way to avoid The Mess- the mess you make of your life, your body, your sanity, by giving everything you have to pleasing people you will never know.” Stay late. Make sacrifices for the company, until you are but a shell of who you once were, and you’re in so deep, like a fishing hook in a finger, and the sunk-cost fallacy has shown its teeth, but you keep your eyes on the light hanging right above its mouth- only to realize that the end of the tunnel is much farther than you know.

    That being said, the artist has never actually worked in a corporate office so what does he know really?

    Hot Water (2024)

    Before her debut as a movie star in the 2026 film “You Can See Everything,” Elizabeth Holmes was better known for her role at the now defunct blood testing company, Theranos. Theranos was founded in 2003 and gained household recognition in 2010 due to claims that it had discovered new methods of blood testing that were both faster and more accurate than existing methods. These claims were false. In 2023, Holmes was convicted of three counts of wire fraud and one count of conspiracy to commit wire fraud. Inside the main bottle of this piece is a shredded copy of U.S. v. Holmes, et al. in keeping with corporate america’s long tradition of shredding damning documents.

    In addition to the commentary on white collar crime, the artist also uses the work to speak to more everyday issues. The office water cooler is a gathering place of sorts, a place where employees can take a much-needed respite from their work to stand around, chat, and drink some water. In the piece, however, the water cooler is overrun with paper, the work following the employees even to their former refuge. This is a representation of the loss of third spaces. A third space is a social setting other than home or work, and is often seen as an important element in fostering community. This work calls to attention the decrease in accessible third spaces due to the current economic crisis, and the overwhelming solitude that can come of this. This work is also one of the artist’s “Professional Pieces,” a series of works co-opting office aesthetics in a satirization of corporate culture.

    worms (2025)

    worms (2025) is an exploration of discomfort through a traditionally soft and comforting medium. Too often is discomfort an emotion shied away from, suppressed. In making this work, the artist encourages the viewer to embrace their discomfort, for there are no good or bad emotions, only emotions. What we see as good and bad are influenced by context.

    The title of the work is multifaceted and amorphous. The implication of worms permeate the work figuratively as much as literally. Do the worms refer to the pink strands dangling from the holes riddled through the suspended form? Or do they refer to the form itself, hanging from a hook as though bait for something larger? There’s always a bigger fish. Perhaps this form serves a function similar to the esca of an anglerfish, luring in unsuspecting prey with the allure of a familiar form. But to truly live, we must on occasion surrender ourselves to the endless abyss of possibility. Embrace the uncomfortable and unknown, the splendor and ecstasy of fear.

    In the modern digital age, too often is discomfort avoided, and more easily than ever. Tiktok, Instagram, Snapchat, Reddit, Quora, Discord, Tumblr, Twitter, Twitch, Facebook, YouTube, Google Giggles, Bluesky, Kick, Linkedin, Jeremy Renner App, Uber, Mastodon, Pintrest, Vine, Rednote, Letterboxd, iFunny, Truth Social, 4chan, Threads, Oloo, Liveleak, WhatsApp, Signal, Tinder, Depop, Telegram, Spotify, Cameo, or ChatGPT: these algorithms will keep you happy and distracted for as long as you let them. More now than ever do we need to train ourselves to pierce the veil of discomfort to escape these prisons of dopamine and serotonin; a paradise perceived, an illusion all the same.
